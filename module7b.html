International Law in Practice

⏱️
Estimated Reading 14 min read

From theory to practice. If Module 7 asked why the law exists, this chapter asks how it is made — the two sources of international law, the mechanics of treaties, the unspoken vocabulary of UN negotiations, and the places where words are quietly bent against the dignity of the person.

Part 1 — Sources of International Law

How is International Law Created?

International law does not fall from the sky. It grows from two distinct roots: the slow accumulation of customary practice, and the deliberate signing of treaties. Both bind states — but they bind them in very different ways.

Reflective Question

If an international rule is binding only because states have historically acted as if it is, what prevents today's behaviour from becoming tomorrow's "law" — for better or for worse?

📜
A) Customary Law

A set of laws based on the traditions, customs, or norms of a local or international community — unwritten, but no less binding.

✍️
B) Treaties

A formally signed and ratified agreement between two or more nations or sovereigns — explicit, written, and consent-based.

A) Customary International Law

The Process of Creating Customary Law

1. Practice
General Practice

Customary law emerges when a rule is consistently followed by a significant number of states over time. There is no fixed number required — but broad and uniform adherence is essential to establishing a legal norm.

Analogy

Like a teenager adopting trends — not because they are required, but because everyone else is doing it.

Real-Life Example

The centuries-long tradition of nations exempting fishing vessels from being captured as prizes of war.

2. Belief
Opinio Juris

This principle exists when states follow a practice out of a sense of legal obligation rather than mere habit. It can be reflected in official statements by state representatives, international organizations, or legal decisions.

Analogy

Like a sister who insists on chocolate cake every birthday — not out of preference, but because she believes it is a tradition she must uphold.

Real-Life Example

Jurists worldwide holding that coast fishing vessels are exempt from capture as prize of war — founded on considerations of humanity and mutual convenience of belligerent states.

B) Treaties

The Process of Creating Treaties

A treaty is a formally signed and ratified agreement between two or more nations or sovereigns. It rests on one principle above all others: consent.

Who has the legal authority to enter into treaties?

Sovereign States

The primary entities that negotiate and become parties to treaties.

International Organizations

Facilitate treaty negotiations and implementation. Examples: the United Nations, the Council of Europe.

Consent — The Foundation of Treaty-Making

1
Signature

Indicates a state's approval of the treaty's principles — but does not create a binding legal obligation.

2
Accession

A state agrees to join a treaty it did not originally negotiate. Formally binding.

3
Ratification

A state formally confirms its commitment to an existing treaty, often following domestic legislative procedures.

Key Concepts in Treaty Law

Reservations

A mechanism that allows states to exclude or modify certain provisions of a treaty while still becoming a party to it. Reservations must be formally submitted and communicated to other parties. Example: Argentina and the Holy See submitted reservations to the Convention on the Rights of the Child, indicating provisions they would not fully accept.

Entry into Force

A treaty becomes legally binding once a specified number of states have expressed their consent, often subject to additional conditions outlined in the treaty text.

Amendment

Treaties may be modified through mutual agreement among the parties. If amendments are made by only some states, they apply exclusively to those states and do not alter the obligations of others.

Checkpoint

Treaty Monitoring Bodies (TMBs)

TMBs are groups of independent experts who monitor countries and issue recommendations with the intention of ensuring that signatory states live up to the treaties they have signed and the reservations they have agreed upon.

Important nuance

Their recommendations are authoritative but not legally binding. They guide compliance; they do not replace the sovereign consent that created the treaty.

🔍
Opinio Juris

"Actions taken or opinions held that are believed to be in accordance with the law, though not actually expressed in the law."

Part 2 — The Vocabulary of Negotiation

Crucial Language in UN Documents

The United Nations operates with a unique set of terms whose definitions are often unstated. Many come to be understood in a particular way over time through use and negotiation. Various combinations of words can carry meanings that are far from what they first appear to say.

Reflective Question

If a single word in a UN document can quietly open the door to a policy that was never openly voted on, how carefully must WYA read every line of every draft?

"

The objective is to ensure that negotiated documents respect and affirm the dignity of the person.

Learning these terms is not an exercise in jargon. It is how WYA defends persons at the drafting table — one word at a time.

A Glossary of Negotiation

Eight Terms, Carefully Defined

💧
1. Health

Understood as basic health care, which includes access to clean water, sanitation, basic health care, and basic nutrition.

🩺
2. Reproductive Health

Gynaecologic and obstetric care, family planning, and treatment for sexually transmitted diseases. It is not understood to include abortion.

⚠️
3. Reproductive Services

The services that are required to achieve reproductive health. No definition includes abortion, but various delegations have publicly stated that they understand it to do so.

🚨
4. Sexual and Reproductive Health Care Services

Serves as an umbrella term for all of the understood provisions each of these terms offers. As such, it can be interpreted (but does not necessarily require) as providing contraceptives, abortifacients, and full access to abortion.

⛔
5. Unsafe Abortion

In UN usage, this term means illegal abortion. The phrasing quietly implies that abortion itself can be made "safe" through legalization.

👨‍👩‍👧
6. The Family / Family

References the traditional family. In North America, Europe, and parts of Far Eastern Asia, this is the nuclear family — parents and children. In Latin America, Africa, South East Asia, and parts of the Pacific Rim, it is the "extended" family — a group of nuclear families across several generations.

👥
7. Various Forms of the Family

Introduced to recognize family situations that embrace inter-generational heads of family, tribal family concepts, and new family arrangements arising as a result of the ravages of HIV/AIDS. The term has also been understood to include the possibility of homosexual unions under the concept of family.

⚧
8. Gender

Refers to the socially constructed roles of individuals, rather than the biological differences between men and women.

Part 3 — Case Studies

Examples of Problematic Language Use

The following excerpts contain language that does not support WYA's understanding of the human person. Read each paragraph carefully — then notice exactly where and how the meaning slips.

Why This Matters

Policy flows from paragraphs. A single ambiguous sentence in an international plan of action can quietly reshape national laws for decades.

Case 1 ICPD Programme of Action, Chapter VI 6.4
"Countries should give greater attention to the importance of population trends for development.1 Countries that have not completed their demographic transition should take effective steps in this regard within the context of their social and economic development and with full respect of human rights.2 Countries that have concluded the demographic transition should take necessary steps to optimize their demographic trends within the context of their social and economic development.3"
Line 1 — The Problem

May be interpreted as "population control equals development" — turning human beings into variables to be optimized rather than persons to be served.

Line 2 — The Problem

May be read as requiring developing countries to direct more resources toward population control rather than toward basic needs such as education, health care, and infrastructure.

Line 3 — The Problem

Frames even developed nations as having to "optimize" their demographic trends — again, people as targets of policy rather than its origin.

Case 2 ICPD Programme of Action, Chapter VII 7.2
"Reproductive health is a state of complete physical, mental, and social well-being and not merely the absence of disease or infirmity, in all matters relating to the reproductive system and to its functions and processes.1 Reproductive health, therefore, implies that people are able to have a satisfying and safe sex life and that they have the capability to reproduce and the freedom to decide if, when and how often to do so.2 Implicit in this last condition are the right of men and women to be informed and to have access to safe, effective, affordable and acceptable methods of family planning of their choice, as well as other methods of their choice for regulation of fertility which are not against the law, and the right of access to appropriate health-care services that will enable women to go safely through pregnancy and childbirth and provide couples with the best chance of having a healthy infant.3"
Line 3 — The Problem

It is not clear what is meant by "acceptable methods of family planning of their choice." Some people could see abortion as an "acceptable" method — even though it may be illegal in their country. The vagueness is the vulnerability.

Case 3 Maputo Plan of Action, 5.1.2a (2006)
5.1.1a Compile and disseminate data on the magnitude and consequences of unsafe abortion.
5.1.2a Enact policies and legal frameworks to reduce incidence of unsafe abortion.
5.1.3a Prepare and implement national plans of action to reduce incidence of unwanted pregnancies and unsafe abortion.

Why would WYA propose replacing "unsafe abortion" with "abortions"?

Reason A

The language equates "unsafe abortion" with illegal abortion, implying that to make abortion "safe" we must legalize it.

Reason B

The phrase "unsafe abortion" implies that abortion itself can ever be made safe — a framing that conceals the harm it does to one of the two persons involved.

The correct answer: A and B. Both framings quietly load the clause against the dignity of the person.

The Discipline of the Negotiator

"

Various combinations of words can provide completely different meanings to initial appearances. The task of WYA is to read every line as if a person's dignity depended on it — because, in international law, it does.
